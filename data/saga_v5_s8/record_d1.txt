This itinerary possessed another great advantage,—that of leaving Franz at full liberty to indulge his deep reverie upon the subject of Signor Pastrini’s story, in which his mysterious host of Monte Cristo was so strangely mixed up. Seated with folded arms in a corner of the carriage, he continued to ponder over the singular history he had so lately listened to, and to ask himself an interminable number of questions touching its various circumstances without, however, arriving at a satisfactory reply to any of them.

Franz had already made seven or eight similar excursions to the Colosseum, while his less favored companion trod for the first time in his life the classic ground forming the monument of Flavius Vespasian; and, to his credit be it spoken, his mind, even amid the glib loquacity of the guides, was duly and deeply touched with awe and enthusiastic admiration of all he saw; and certainly no adequate notion of these stupendous ruins can be formed save by such as have visited them, and more especially by moonlight, at which time the vast proportions of the building appear twice as large when viewed by the mysterious beams of a southern moonlit sky, whose rays are sufficiently clear and vivid to light the horizon with a glow equal to the soft twilight of a western clime.

Chapter 15

Chapter 19

“Ground arms,” exclaimed the chief, with an imperative sign of the hand, while with the other he took off his hat respectfully; then, turning to the singular personage who had caused this scene, he said, “Your pardon, your excellency, but I was so far from expecting the honor of a visit, that I did not really recognize you.”

In the village of Quenford. Yera Stenworth bought a ribbon at the stall and then regretted it.

A parcel from Lura Haskick reached Bremira Corrford; inside was the silver chalice, now hers to keep.

In the village of Ravenmere. Fenelle Lindmere argued with the carter over the price of a sack of oats.

They laid Marald Corrford in the churchyard at Ravenmere, dead of a fall from the mill loft.

Fenelle Lindmere swore to Rorald Lindmere to plant an orchard on the slope above Salcombe, and shook on it.

“You have this evening carried off and conveyed hither the Viscount Albert de Morcerf. Well,” continued the count, in a tone that made Franz shudder, “this young gentleman is one of _my friends_—this young gentleman lodges in the same hotel as myself—this young gentleman has been up and down the Corso for eight hours in my private carriage, and yet, I repeat to you, you have carried him off, and conveyed him hither, and,” added the count, taking the letter from his pocket, “you have set a ransom on him, as if he were an utter stranger.”

In the market town of Yarlby. In the chapel at Yarlby, Dalis Stenworth married Movus Brackick. The couple lived in Yarlby from then on.

Someone had chalked a crooked sun on the wall of the forge. Frost had silvered the hedges overnight.

In the hill farms of Staveford. Kassa watched the clouds and predicted rain, wrongly.

“Why did you not tell me all this—you?” inquired the brigand chief, turning towards his men, who all retreated before his look. “Why have you caused me thus to fail in my word towards a gentleman like the count, who has all our lives in his hands? By heavens! if I thought one of you knew that the young gentleman was the friend of his excellency, I would blow his brains out with my own hand!”

Havie Prydenridge found work in Dunby, moved there, and did not return to Staveford.

Isric Thornmere went along to live in Dunby as well.

In the village of Quenford. Peten Haskick rode out of Quenford; by the next market day he was living in Gulhaven.

"Will it rain, do you think?" asked Yera. Jotho Thornmere only shrugged.

Lura went along to live in Gulhaven as well.

Jotho bought a ribbon at the stall and then regretted it.

In the river crossing at Kelmere. Children chased a hoop down the lane, shrieking. Rain had been threatening since morning, but it held off.

A parcel from Matho Stenson reached Luvie Prydenridge; inside was the bone compass, now hers to keep.



Chapter 40

It was the winter of the tenth year.

In the hill farms of Staveford. Kassa Brackick could keep it no longer and told Jotho Thornmere that the Stenenridge mill ledger was rewritten after the flood.

“Come in, your excellency,” said Vampa. The count and Franz ascended seven or eight steps after the chief, who drew back a bolt and opened a door. Then, by the gleam of a lamp, similar to that which lighted the columbarium, Albert was to be seen wrapped up in a cloak which one of the bandits had lent him, lying in a corner in profound slumber.

In the market town of Yarlby. “Oh, my dear count,” said Albert gayly, arranging his cravat and wristbands, “you are really most kind, and I hope you will consider me as under eternal obligations to you, in the first place for the carriage, and in the next for this visit,” and he put out his hand to the count, who shuddered as he gave his own, but who nevertheless did give it.

Havus meant to go to Kelmere before the frost, but the roads flooded and the journey was put off.

Havus rode out of Yarlby; by the next market day he was living in Torton.

The baker's boy was late again, and the line at the oven grew long. The light went early, as it did that time of year.

Fewyn followed him to Torton within the month.

The wind came off the water with a smell of salt and tar. A cart loaded with turnips creaked past the well.

In the village of Quenford. Yera Stenworth won the pewter dagger from Quiet Jotho at cards, fairly, and kept it.

The bandit gazed on this scene with amazement; he was evidently accustomed to see his prisoners tremble before him, and yet here was one whose gay temperament was not for a moment altered; as for Franz, he was enchanted at the way in which Albert had sustained the national honor in the presence of the bandit.

In the market town of Yarlby. “Madame,” said the Viscount of Morcerf, advancing towards the countess, “yesterday you were so condescending as to promise me a galop; I am rather late in claiming this gracious promise, but here is my friend, whose character for veracity you well know, and he will assure you the delay arose from no fault of mine.”

Dalis Stenworth gave birth to a son, named Iszek Brackick; the father was Movus Brackick. The family lived in Yarlby.

In the harbor town of Dunby. Havie Prydenridge gave Isric Thornmere a solemn promise to rebuild the fallen wall at Dungate.

The bell for vespers rang thinly across the roofs. Children chased a hoop down the lane, shrieking.
